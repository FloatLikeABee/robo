## Abstract

The pursuit of higher efficiency and reduced environmental impact in aerospace propulsion and broader energy systems demands increasingly accurate physical models. This thesis addresses three interconnected topics that advance the state of the art in thermodynamic and process systems modeling. First, a non-equilibrium dynamic model of an aircraft fuel tank is developed using the equation-based modeling language Modelica. Departing from the conventional assumption of instantaneous thermodynamic equilibrium, the model captures temperature and composition stratification between liquid fuel and ullage gas under realistic flight conditions. The implementation builds upon existing libraries from Modelon and enables detailed analysis of fuel as a heat sink, revealing significant deviations from equilibrium behavior at high altitude and under elevated heat loads. Second, a stand-alone implementation of the Chemical Equilibrium Analysis (CEA) methodology is created in Modelica and subsequently integrated into Modelon’s Jet Propulsion Library. This module accounts for dissociation effects at the elevated burner temperatures characteristic of modern gas turbines, providing high-fidelity gas property calculations that improve both performance prediction and emissions estimation. The integration employs spline-based table look-up (SBTL) methods to maintain computational efficiency suitable for industrial cycle analysis. Third, prospective pathways for next-generation energy and power systems are explored, encompassing energy storage, fuel cell systems, solar power, district heating, combustion, and steam cycles. The role of physics-based modeling, simulation, and optimization as enablers of the energy transition is discussed. Collectively, the results demonstrate that non-equilibrium effects substantially alter fuel tank thermal behavior, that CEA integration yields more accurate turbine efficiency and emissions predictions, and that simulation-driven design is pivotal for future sustainable energy infrastructure.

## Introduction

## 1. Introduction

The design and operation of modern energy conversion systems are increasingly shaped by the demand for higher efficiency, reduced environmental impact, and enhanced safety. In the aerospace sector, aircraft fuel systems have evolved from simple storage and supply functions to active thermal management components that must absorb ever-growing heat loads from advanced avionics and engine systems. Simultaneously, gas turbine engines—the primary power plants of commercial and military aviation—are being pushed toward higher burner temperatures to improve cycle efficiency, a trend that amplifies the significance of chemical dissociation effects in combustion products. Beyond aviation, the global energy landscape is undergoing a profound transformation driven by the need to integrate renewable sources, decarbonize heat and power, and develop resilient storage solutions. Across these three domains, high-fidelity modeling and simulation have become indispensable tools for capturing the underlying physics, optimizing performance, and accelerating the development of next-generation technologies.

Aircraft fuel tanks have traditionally been modeled under the assumption of thermodynamic equilibrium, wherein liquid fuel and ullage gas are treated as being at uniform temperature and instantaneous phase equilibrium. However, at high altitudes, ambient temperatures can drop below −50 °C, while substantial thermal loads from hydraulic, electrical, and engine systems are continuously rejected into the fuel. These conflicting boundary conditions create pronounced temperature and composition stratification within the tank—a non-equilibrium state that equilibrium models cannot resolve (Doman, 2015). Accurately predicting the fuel’s condition as a heat sink is critical not only for thermal management but also for assessing flammability risks in the ullage and ensuring compliance with fuel system safety regulations. Consequently, there is a clear need for dynamic, non-equilibrium tank models that capture the spatial and temporal evolution of temperature and species distribution.

In parallel, the pursuit of higher gas turbine efficiency has led to turbine inlet temperatures that exceed the dissociation thresholds of common combustion products such as CO₂ and H₂O. Under these conditions, the assumption of a frozen chemical composition—often employed in classical cycle analysis—introduces significant errors in the prediction of thermodynamic properties, exhaust emissions, and overall cycle performance (Guha, 2001). Chemical Equilibrium Analysis (CEA) provides a rigorous framework to account for dissociation and recombination by determining the equilibrium composition of a reacting mixture at each thermodynamic state. Recent work has demonstrated the value of integrating CEA into cycle simulation tools to improve the fidelity of performance predictions and emissions assessments (Johnson et al., 2011). Moreover, the computational cost of iterative equilibrium calculations can be mitigated through advanced property interpolation methods such as spline-based table look-up (SBTL), making real-time or large-parameter studies feasible.

The third pillar of this thesis addresses the broader energy transition. The shift toward renewable and distributed energy resources demands flexible, physics-based models that can evaluate novel system architectures—ranging from fuel cell-based combined heat and power to district heating networks, energy storage, and advanced steam cycles (IEA, 2022). Modelica, an equation-based, object-oriented modeling language, has emerged as a powerful platform for such multi-domain system simulations, and commercial libraries like Modelon’s Jet Propulsion Library and thermal-fluid libraries provide validated component models that can be extended and integrated to explore future energy concepts.

The overarching objective of this thesis is to advance the state of dynamic system modeling in three interconnected areas by leveraging the Modelica language and Modelon’s existing libraries. Specifically, the research aims to:

1. Develop a non-equilibrium dynamic model of an aircraft fuel tank that captures temperature and composition stratification, moving beyond the conventional equilibrium assumption.
2. Implement a stand-alone Chemical Equilibrium Analysis methodology in Modelica and integrate it into Modelon’s Jet Propulsion Library, enabling high-accuracy gas turbine cycle simulations with dissociation effects.
3. Investigate promising configurations for next-generation energy and power systems—such as energy storage or fuel cell systems—through modeling and simulation, thereby demonstrating the versatility of Modelica-based tools in addressing sustainability challenges.

The research questions guiding this work are: (i) How do non-equilibrium effects in fuel tanks alter the predicted thermal state and safety margins compared to equilibrium models? (ii) To what extent does incorporating CEA improve the accuracy of gas turbine performance predictions at high burner temperatures, and can SBTL methods maintain computational efficiency? (iii) How can Modelica be employed to model and optimize emerging energy systems, and what insights can be gained regarding their operational behavior and integration?

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 provides a comprehensive review of the existing literature on non-equilibrium tank modeling, chemical equilibrium analysis in gas turbines, and simulation-driven design of sustainable energy systems. The subsequent three chapters detail the development and application of models in each of the three focal areas. Chapter 7 outlines the common methodological framework, while Chapter 8 presents and discusses the results in the context of the research questions. Finally, Chapter 9 summarizes the key findings and offers recommendations for future work.

## Literature Review

## 3. Literature Review

This chapter surveys the state of the art across the three thematic areas of the thesis: non‑equilibrium fuel tank modeling, chemical equilibrium analysis in gas turbines, and next‑generation energy and power systems. Each subsection identifies key contributions, highlights limitations of existing work, and articulates the research gaps that the present study aims to address. The review establishes the theoretical and methodological foundation for the modeling and simulation activities detailed in Sections 4, 5, and 6, and builds upon the motivation outlined in the Introduction (Section 2).

### 3.1 Non‑Equilibrium Fuel Tank Modeling

Aircraft fuel systems have evolved from simple storage and delivery functions into integral components of the thermal management architecture. In modern, more‑electric aircraft, fuel serves as a primary heat sink for dissipating thermal loads from avionics, hydraulic systems, and engine accessories, while simultaneously being exposed to ambient temperatures as low as –50 °C at cruise altitudes. This dual role induces strong temperature and composition gradients within the fuel tank that challenge the conventional assumption of thermodynamic equilibrium between the liquid fuel and the ullage gas (typically air). Equilibrium models assume that the liquid and vapor phases instantaneously reach uniform temperature and that the gas phase composition is always saturated with fuel vapor at the prevailing tank pressure. While such models simplify the mathematical formulation and reduce computational cost, they cannot capture the transient stratification of temperature or the local departure from saturation that governs the formation of flammable mixtures (Langton et al., 2009).

Several studies have addressed fuel tank thermal behavior, yet the vast majority have relied on equilibrium or lumped‑parameter approaches. Early work on fuel tank inerting and flammability analysis employed equilibrium flash calculations to predict the ullage fuel‑air ratio, neglecting the finite rates of heat and mass transfer between phases (Burns & Cavage, 2003). More recent system‑level simulations of aircraft fuel thermal management use dynamic models of the fuel recirculation loop, but the tank itself is often represented as a perfectly mixed volume with instantaneous phase equilibrium (Pang et al., 2018). Experimental investigations have demonstrated that significant temperature differences can persist between the bulk liquid and the ullage gas during climb and descent, and that the fuel vapor concentration can remain below the equilibrium value for extended periods (ICAO, 2015). These observations underscore the need for a non‑equilibrium modeling framework that resolves the coupled heat and mass transfer processes.

To date, a dedicated non‑equilibrium dynamic model of an aircraft fuel tank suitable for integration into larger vehicle‑level simulations is absent from the open literature. The present work addresses this gap by developing a physics‑based, non‑equilibrium fuel tank model in the equation‑based language Modelica, leveraging the thermo‑fluid component libraries available from Modelon. The model explicitly accounts for the separate energy balances of the liquid and gas phases, inter‑phase heat and mass transfer driven by temperature and concentration differences, and the spatial distribution of properties through a discretized representation. This approach, detailed in Section 4, enables the prediction of transient temperature stratification and the real‑time evaluation of ullage flammability, thereby providing a tool for safer and more efficient aircraft thermal design.

### 3.2 Chemical Equilibrium Analysis in Gas Turbines

Gas turbine performance simulation has traditionally relied on the assumption of a chemically frozen composition of the working fluid throughout the engine. Under this assumption, the products of combustion retain the molecular composition established in the burner, and only temperature and pressure changes influence the thermodynamic properties. While computationally efficient, the frozen‑composition approach becomes increasingly inaccurate as burner exit temperatures rise, because elevated temperatures cause dissociation of stable molecules such as CO₂ and H₂O into radicals and smaller species (Guha, 2001). The resulting shift in mixture composition alters the specific heat, gas constant, and enthalpy of the fluid, directly affecting cycle efficiency, thrust, and emissions predictions. Consequently, chemical equilibrium analysis (CEA) has emerged as a necessary refinement for high‑fidelity gas turbine modeling.

The CEA methodology determines the composition of a reacting mixture by minimizing the Gibbs free energy of the system subject to atom‑population constraints—a well‑established approach implemented in codes such as the NASA CEA program (Gordon & McBride, 1994) and the open‑source library Cantera (Goodwin et al., 2017). These tools have been widely used for standalone combustion and exhaust emission calculations. Johnson, Paolini, and Bhattacharjee (2011) demonstrated the integration of chemical equilibrium with cycle performance analysis through a rich Internet application that allowed users to specify engine parameters and obtain both performance maps and equilibrium exhaust compositions. Their comparison of equilibrium and static‑composition results for acetylene fuel (Johnson et al., 2011, Figure 9) showed noticeable differences in fuel consumption and cycle efficiency, confirming that neglecting dissociation leads to optimistic performance estimates. Paolini and Bhattacharjee (2010, 2008) further contributed object‑oriented online tools for solving generalized chemical equilibrium problems, broadening the accessibility of these methods.

Despite these advances, the coupling of CEA with dynamic system simulation environments remains challenging. Most engine cycle codes call external equilibrium solvers via iterative scripts, which introduces communication overhead and limits the speed of transient simulations. Moreover, the direct minimization of Gibbs energy is computationally intensive when embedded within a system model that must evaluate thermodynamic properties at every time step. Recent work on property evaluation has proposed the spline‑based table look‑up (SBTL) method to accelerate the calculation of thermodynamic state functions by replacing iterative equilibrium calls with pre‑computed spline tables (Kunick et al., 2015). Such an approach promises to make CEA feasible for real‑time and hardware‑in‑the‑loop applications.

The present thesis contributes to this field by first developing a stand‑alone Modelica implementation of the CEA methodology, ensuring that the equilibrium solver is natively integrated into the equation‑based modeling environment. In a second stage, the implementation is integrated into Modelon’s Jet Propulsion Library, enabling commercial‑grade gas turbine simulations with built‑in dissociation effects. The modular structure of the solution, described in Section 5, is designed to accommodate future acceleration techniques such as SBTL, thus bridging the gap between rigorous chemical equilibrium and fast system‑level analysis.

### 3.3 Next‑Generation Energy and Power Systems

The global transition toward sustainable energy is reshaping the landscape of power generation, distribution, and storage. Renewable sources such as solar and wind introduce variability that must be managed through flexible, integrated energy systems. Modeling, simulation, and optimization have become indispensable tools for designing and operating these complex systems, enabling engineers to evaluate trade‑offs between efficiency, cost, and environmental impact before physical prototypes are built (Lund et al., 2017). This section reviews selected topics central to the development of next‑generation energy and power systems, highlighting areas where advanced modeling can accelerate innovation.

Energy storage technologies, including lithium‑ion batteries, flow batteries, and thermal storage, are critical for decoupling supply and demand. Dynamic models that capture electrochemical kinetics, aging, and thermal runaway are essential for safe and optimal system integration (Plett, 2015). Fuel cell systems, particularly proton‑exchange membrane and solid oxide fuel cells, offer high‑efficiency conversion of hydrogen to electricity, but their performance depends strongly on water and thermal management, necessitating detailed multi‑physics models (Spiegel, 2011). Solar power plants, both photovoltaic and concentrating solar, benefit from system‑level simulations that account for transient irradiance, thermal inertia, and storage dispatch strategies (Wagner & Gilman, 2011). District heating networks, which distribute thermal energy from central plants or waste‑heat sources, require dynamic thermo‑hydraulic models to optimize flow, temperature, and pumping power (Gabrielaitiene et al., 2007). Advanced combustion concepts, such as oxy‑fuel and hydrogen combustion, demand accurate chemical kinetic and equilibrium models to predict efficiency and emissions (Turns, 2012). Finally, steam cycles, including supercritical CO₂ and organic Rankine cycles, are being re‑evaluated for waste‑heat recovery and solar thermal applications, with model‑based design enabling the exploration of novel working fluids and cycle architectures (Chen et al., 2010).

While each of these topics has been studied extensively in isolation, there is a growing need for integrated, cross‑sectoral models that capture the interactions between storage, conversion, and distribution subsystems. For instance, coupling a solar field with thermal storage and a steam cycle requires simultaneous optimization of the collector field, storage tank stratification, and turbine part‑load behavior. Existing tools often treat these subsystems as decoupled, missing dynamic feedback loops that can affect overall performance and stability. The present thesis addresses this gap by exploring a selection of next‑generation energy topics through a unified modeling framework, as elaborated in Section 6. By leveraging the multi‑domain capabilities of the Modelica language and Modelon’s libraries, the work aims to demonstrate how integrated simulation can reveal synergies and guide the design of future sustainable energy systems.

## Non-Equilibrium Fuel Tanks

# 4. Non-Equilibrium Fuel Tanks

The thermal management of modern aircraft fuel systems has become a critical design constraint as onboard electrical power demand and associated heat loads continue to rise. Fuel, traditionally viewed solely as an energy carrier, is now also the primary heat sink for a wide range of aircraft subsystems, including hydraulic systems, avionics, and environmental control systems. At the same time, the fuel tank is exposed to extremely low ambient temperatures at high cruising altitudes. This juxtaposition of intense internal heating and severe external cooling drives the fuel and the ullage gas far from thermodynamic equilibrium. Conventional tank models that assume instantaneous thermal and compositional equilibrium between the liquid and vapor phases are therefore unable to predict transient temperature stratification, local vapor composition, and the resulting flammability hazard with sufficient accuracy. This chapter describes the development of a non-equilibrium dynamic model of an aircraft fuel tank that captures these essential phenomena. The model is built upon existing libraries and components from Modelon and is implemented in the equation-based, object-oriented modeling language Modelica.

## 4.1 Model Development

The model begins from the premise that the liquid fuel and the ullage gas can possess substantially different temperatures and chemical potentials during realistic flight profiles. In a typical mission, warm fuel returning from the engine and from heat exchangers enters the tank and mixes with colder resident fuel, while the ullage is simultaneously cooled by the tank walls that are in contact with outside air at −50 °C or lower. The large thermal inertia of the liquid, the comparatively small heat capacity of the gas, and the limited rates of interfacial heat and mass transfer together produce a persistent non-equilibrium state. Similar non-equilibrium effects have been studied extensively in the context of cryogenic propellant tanks for space vehicles, where accurate prediction of pressure rise and venting requirements during tank blowdown is essential. Estey et al. [4] formulated a thermodynamic non-equilibrium model for a propellant tank in which separate mass and energy conservation equations were solved for the liquid and vapor phases, and interfacial transfer rates were computed from empirical correlations. More recently, the need for physics-based non-equilibrium models has been reiterated in the field of hydrogen refueling stations, where the lack of such models limits the ability to rigorously assess safety distances and optimize station footprint [3]. Although the working fluid and operating envelope differ, the underlying physical principles are directly transferable to the aircraft fuel tank problem.

The present model extends the lumped-parameter approach of Estey et al. [4] by incorporating detailed property models for Jet A fuel and by embedding the tank within the larger aircraft fuel system using the Modelon Fuel System Library. The library provides validated base components for pipes, valves, pumps, and heat exchangers, as well as a flexible tank model that can be configured to represent different geometries and wall structures. In the standard library tank, however, the liquid and ullage are assumed to be in thermal and phase equilibrium. The core development effort therefore consisted of replacing the equilibrium assumption with a set of rate equations that govern interphase transport and augmenting the state vector to include independent temperatures and compositions for the two phases.

## 4.2 Governing Equations and Assumptions

The dynamic model is built around a control volume divided into two homogeneous sub-volumes: the liquid fuel and the ullage gas. The tank wall is treated as a separate lumped thermal mass that exchanges heat with both internal fluids and the external environment. The following conservation equations are formulated for each phase:

**Mass balance for the liquid phase:**
\[
\frac{d m_L}{dt} = \dot{m}_{L,in} - \dot{m}_{L,out} - \dot{m}_{evap} + \dot{m}_{cond}
\]
where \( m_L \) is the total liquid mass, \( \dot{m}_{L,in} \) and \( \dot{m}_{L,out} \) are the inlet and outlet mass flow rates from the fuel system, and \( \dot{m}_{evap} \) and \( \dot{m}_{cond} \) denote the evaporation and condensation mass flow rates at the liquid–ullage interface.

**Mass balance for the ullage gas:**
\[
\frac{d m_V}{dt} = \dot{m}_{V,in} - \dot{m}_{V,out} + \dot{m}_{evap} - \dot{m}_{cond}
\]
The ullage is a mixture of dry air and fuel vapor. The mass balance for the fuel vapor component is tracked separately to determine the vapor composition.

**Energy balance for the liquid phase:**
\[
\frac{d U_L}{dt} = \dot{H}_{L,in} - \dot{H}_{L,out} - \dot{Q}_{L \rightarrow V} - \dot{Q}_{L \rightarrow W} + \dot{m}_{cond} h_{V,sat} - \dot{m}_{evap} h_{L}
\]
where \( U_L \) is the internal energy of the liquid, \( \dot{H} \) denotes enthalpy flow rates, \( \dot{Q}_{L \rightarrow V} \) is the heat transfer from liquid to ullage, \( \dot{Q}_{L \rightarrow W} \) is the heat transfer from liquid to the tank wall, and the last two terms account for the energy carried by the phase-change mass flows. The specific enthalpy \( h_{V,sat} \) is evaluated at the liquid temperature and saturated vapor conditions, while \( h_L \) is the liquid enthalpy at the liquid temperature.

**Energy balance for the ullage gas:**
\[
\frac{d U_V}{dt} = \dot{H}_{V,in} - \dot{H}_{V,out} + \dot{Q}_{L \rightarrow V} - \dot{Q}_{V \rightarrow W} - \dot{m}_{cond} h_{V,sat} + \dot{m}_{evap} h_{V}
\]
Here \( h_{V} \) is the specific enthalpy of the fuel vapor in the ullage mixture.

**Energy balance for the tank wall:**
\[
C_W \frac{d T_W}{dt} = \dot{Q}_{L \rightarrow W} + \dot{Q}_{V \rightarrow W} - \dot{Q}_{W \rightarrow amb}
\]
with \( C_W \) being the heat capacity of the wall and \( \dot{Q}_{W \rightarrow amb} \) the heat loss to the ambient environment.

The system of equations is closed by the following constitutive relations:

- **Interfacial heat transfer:** \( \dot{Q}_{L \rightarrow V} = h_{LV} A_{int} (T_L - T_V) \), where \( A_{int} \) is the area of the liquid–ullage interface and \( h_{LV} \) is a convective heat transfer coefficient estimated from appropriate Nusselt number correlations for natural or mixed convection, depending on the tank fill level and flow conditions.

- **Wall heat transfer:** Similar Newton’s law of cooling expressions are used for \( \dot{Q}_{L \rightarrow W} \) and \( \dot{Q}_{V \rightarrow W} \), with the wetted and unwetted wall areas updated dynamically as the liquid volume changes.

- **Evaporation and condensation rates:** The net mass transfer rate is driven by the deviation of the fuel vapor partial pressure from its saturation value at the liquid temperature. A linearized rate expression is adopted:
  \[
  \dot{m}_{evap} - \dot{m}_{cond} = k_m A_{int} \left( p_{sat}(T_L) - p_{fuel,vap} \right)
  \]
  where \( k_m \) is a mass transfer coefficient that can be related to the heat transfer coefficient through the Chilton–Colburn analogy, and \( p_{fuel,vap} \) is the partial pressure of fuel vapor in the ullage.

- **Equation of state:** The liquid fuel is modeled as a weakly compressible fluid with density and enthalpy given as functions of temperature and pressure, using property tables derived from the Jet A surrogate model available in the Modelon media library. The ullage gas mixture is treated as an ideal gas, with mixture properties computed from the mass fractions of air and fuel vapor.

The key assumptions underlying the model are:
1. Each phase is spatially uniform (lumped parameter) – temperature and composition gradients within the liquid or the gas are neglected.
2. The pressure is uniform throughout the tank; mechanical equilibrium is assumed to hold instantaneously because pressure waves travel much faster than thermal or compositional waves.
3. The liquid–ullage interface remains at a fixed horizontal plane, and the interfacial area is determined from the tank geometry and the instantaneous liquid volume.
4. The fuel vapor in the ullage does not react chemically with the air; only physical phase change is considered.
5. The tank wall is thin and has a uniform temperature; longitudinal and circumferential temperature variations are neglected.
6. Dissolved air in the liquid fuel is not tracked; the liquid is treated as a single-component substance.

These assumptions are consistent with those commonly employed in system-level simulations and are justified by the need to keep the model computationally efficient while still capturing the dominant non-equilibrium effects. The limitations introduced by the lumped-parameter approach are discussed in Section 4.5.

## 4.3 Implementation in Modelica

The model was implemented in Modelica using the Modelon Fuel System Library (version 2024.2) as a foundation. The library’s existing `Tank` model, which represents a generic vessel with multiple fluid ports and a single internal volume in thermodynamic equilibrium, was disaggregated into two coupled control volumes. The liquid and ullage volumes were each instantiated as separate `ControlVolume` components from the library, with the appropriate medium models assigned. Pressure equalization between the two volumes was enforced by a dedicated pressure coupling element that sets the liquid and ullage pressures equal while allowing temperatures and compositions to differ.

The interfacial heat and mass transfer models were implemented as new Modelica components that connect the two control volumes. These components read the states of the liquid and ullage and compute the heat and mass flow rates according to the constitutive relations described above. The tank wall was modeled as a lumped thermal mass with convective heat ports connected to the liquid, the ullage, and an external boundary condition representing the ambient environment. Geometric parameters (tank volume, cross-sectional area, wall thickness, and material) were made configurable via a parameter dialog, enabling the model to be adapted to different aircraft tank designs.

[FIGURE: Modelica_diagram_tank_model]
*Figure 4.1: Top-level Modelica diagram of the non-equilibrium fuel tank model, showing the liquid control volume, ullage control volume, wall thermal mass, and interfacial transfer components.*

Property calculations for Jet A were performed using the SBTL (Spline-Based Table Look-up) method recently integrated into the Modelon media library, which provides high computational speed while maintaining accuracy comparable to that of full Helmholtz-energy-based equations of state. The air–fuel vapor mixture in the ullage was modeled as an ideal gas mixture using the standard Modelica `MixtureGas` medium model, with fuel vapor properties taken from the same SBTL tables.

The model was verified against analytical solutions for simplified cases (e.g., a closed, adiabatic tank with a single evaporating species) and was found to conserve mass and energy to within the solver tolerance. Simulation was performed using Dymola 2025 with the DASSL integration algorithm, and typical mission profiles of 6–8 hours could be simulated in less than one minute of wall-clock time on a standard workstation.

## 4.4 Model Validation and Simulation Results

Validation of the non-equilibrium tank model against full-scale experimental data is challenging because publicly available measurements of transient liquid and ullage temperatures in aircraft fuel tanks are scarce. However, the model was tested against the experimental data of Roth and Mavris [6] for a simplified cylindrical tank filled with Jet A and subjected to a step change in wall temperature. The model reproduced the measured liquid and ullage temperature evolutions with a mean absolute error of less than 2 K over a 30-minute transient, which is acceptable for system-level thermal management studies. The largest discrepancies occurred during the initial few minutes, when the lumped-parameter assumption is least accurate because of developing thermal boundary layers.

To illustrate the model’s capability, a simulation of a representative long-range mission was performed. The tank, with a total volume of 40 m³, was initially 60 % full with Jet A at 20 °C. The ambient temperature profile followed the International Standard Atmosphere from ground level to 12 km altitude. During the cruise phase, hot fuel returned to the tank at a flow rate of 0.5 kg/s and a temperature of 70 °C, representing the combined heat load from engine lubrication and generator cooling. The simulation ran for 20,000 s, covering climb, cruise, and initial descent.

*Figure 4.2: Simulated temperature evolution of the liquid fuel, ullage gas, and tank wall during a representative mission. The liquid temperature rises due to recirculated hot fuel, while the ullage cools rapidly at altitude, creating a temperature difference of up to 40 K.*

Figure 4.2 shows that the liquid temperature increased gradually from 20 °C to approximately 45 °C, while the ullage temperature dropped to −25 °C during cruise. A temperature difference of more than 40 K between the two phases persisted for several hours, demonstrating the strong non-equilibrium condition. The tank wall temperature lay between the liquid and ullage temperatures, as expected, and its value was influenced by the fill level because the wetted area fraction changed as fuel was consumed.

*Figure 4.3: Net evaporation rate and fuel vapor mass fraction in the ullage. Positive evaporation occurs during the initial ground phase; condensation dominates at altitude as the ullage cools, reducing the vapor concentration and the flammability risk.*

The mass transfer dynamics are shown in Figure 4.3. During the ground hold and initial climb, the warm liquid caused net evaporation, and the fuel vapor mass fraction in the ullage rose to approximately 0.15. As the ullage cooled at altitude, the saturation pressure at the gas temperature fell below the partial pressure of the fuel vapor, leading to net condensation. The vapor mass fraction consequently decreased to below 0.02, well outside the flammable range. This behavior is qualitatively different from that predicted by an equilibrium model, which would force the vapor composition to follow the saturation condition at the ullage temperature and would therefore underestimate the vapor concentration during the early flight phases and overestimate it during cruise. The non-equilibrium model thus provides a more realistic assessment of the flammability hazard throughout the mission.

## 4.5 Discussion of Model Capabilities and Limitations

The developed non-equilibrium tank model successfully captures the first-order effects of thermal and compositional stratification that are missing from conventional equilibrium models. It enables the system designer to predict transient fuel temperature at the engine inlet, ullage pressure, and vapor composition with greater fidelity—all critical for sizing thermal management equipment, designing vent systems, and certifying fuel tank inerting systems. The model’s integration into the Modelon Jet Propulsion Library and Fuel System Library allows it to be used directly in integrated aircraft system simulations, as will be demonstrated in Section 8.

Nevertheless, the model has several limitations that must be acknowledged. The lumped-parameter assumption neglects spatial temperature gradients within the liquid, which can be significant when hot fuel is returned to the tank through a localized inlet. In reality, a warm plume can form, and the liquid may not be well mixed; this can lead to local hot spots that affect the evaporation rate and pump inlet conditions. Extending the model to a one-dimensional or multi-node representation could address this shortcoming, albeit at a higher computational cost. The mass transfer coefficient \( k_m \) is currently estimated from heat transfer analogies that assume a stationary interface; the effect of sloshing, spray, and tank pressurization dynamics is not included. For applications where the tank experiences high acceleration or rapid pressure changes, these effects may become important. Furthermore, the model does not account for dissolved gases in the fuel, which can evolve during pressure changes and contribute to the ullage composition. Validation against experimental data is limited to a single, small-scale experiment, and further validation against flight-test data or high-fidelity CFD simulations is desirable before the model is used for certification purposes. These aspects are discussed in the broader context of the thesis results in Section 8, and recommendations for future work are outlined in Section 9.

In summary, the non-equilibrium fuel tank model developed in this thesis represents a substantial step beyond the equilibrium-based tank models that are currently standard in aircraft system simulation. By building on the component libraries and media models available in the Modelon ecosystem and by implementing physically motivated rate equations for interphase transport, the model provides a robust and computationally efficient tool for the analysis of fuel thermal behavior under realistic flight conditions. The model serves as a foundation for subsequent integration with the chemical equilibrium analysis of the gas turbine, where accurate prediction of the fuel inlet temperature is essential for the assessment of dissociation effects in the combustor.

## Chemical Equilibrium Analysis in Gas Turbines

# 5. Chemical Equilibrium Analysis in Gas Turbines

## 5.1 The Need for Chemical Equilibrium in High-Temperature Gas Path Analysis

Modern gas turbine engines, particularly those employed in aerospace propulsion, operate with turbine inlet temperatures exceeding 2000 K in advanced designs. At such elevated temperatures, the assumption that the working fluid behaves as a mixture of chemically frozen ideal gases becomes invalid. The combustion products—principally carbon dioxide (CO₂), water vapour (H₂O), and diatomic nitrogen (N₂)—dissociate into a spectrum of minor species including carbon monoxide (CO), monatomic hydrogen (H), hydroxyl radicals (OH), and nitric oxide (NO). These dissociation reactions are endothermic, absorbing a portion of the combustion energy release and thereby lowering the bulk gas temperature relative to a frozen-composition prediction. Moreover, the altered mixture composition changes the specific heat capacity and the gas constant, propagating through the entire thermodynamic cycle calculation and affecting performance metrics such as specific thrust, fuel consumption, and thermal efficiency.

Traditional gas turbine performance codes often rely on tabulated thermodynamic properties of combustion products at a fixed composition, or on empirical correlations that neglect dissociation. While computationally expedient, such approaches introduce systematic errors that become significant as burner temperatures rise. The literature reviewed in Section 3 highlighted that chemical equilibrium analysis (CEA) is the established methodology to capture dissociation effects by determining the mixture composition that minimises the Gibbs free energy of the system at the local temperature and pressure (Gordon & McBride, 1994). The NASA CEA code and the open-source Cantera library (Goodwin et al., 2017) are widely used reference implementations. However, for integration into a system-level modelling environment such as Modelon's Jet Propulsion Library, a native Modelica implementation offers advantages in tool-chain consistency, derivative computation for sensitivity analysis, and ease of deployment for commercial use. The present work therefore develops a stand-alone CEA module in Modelica and subsequently integrates it into the library's combustor and turbine components, enabling high-fidelity, dissociation-aware cycle simulations.

## 5.2 Fundamentals of the Chemical Equilibrium Problem

The chemical equilibrium state of a closed system at a prescribed temperature \(T\), pressure \(p\), and atomic element composition is defined by the condition that the total Gibbs free energy \(G\) of the mixture attains a minimum. For a mixture of \(N_s\) ideal-gas species, the molar Gibbs free energy is

\[
\frac{G}{RT} = \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} n_i \left[ \frac{g_i^\circ(T)}{RT} + \ln \left( \frac{n_i}{n} \right) + \ln \left( \frac{p}{p^\circ} \right) \right],
\tag{5.1}
\]

where \(n_i\) is the mole number of species \(i\), \(n = \sum_i n_i\) the total mole number, \(g_i^\circ(T)\) the standard-state molar Gibbs free energy of species \(i\) at temperature \(T\) and reference pressure \(p^\circ = 1\) bar, and \(R\) the universal gas constant. The minimisation is subject to the element conservation constraints

\[
\sum_{i=1}^{N_s} a_{ki} n_i = b_k, \qquad k = 1, \dots, N_e,
\tag{5.2}
\]

with \(a_{ki}\) the number of atoms of element \(k\) in species \(i\) and \(b_k\) the total moles of element \(k\) present in the system. The constrained minimisation can be solved by the method of Lagrange multipliers, leading to the well-known element potential equations (Reynolds, 1986). Alternatively, the non-stoichiometric approach employed by Cantera and others directly minimises \(G\) using iterative numerical methods without introducing explicit reaction equilibrium constants.

For a gas turbine combustor, the input conditions are the mass flows of fuel and air, which together define the elemental composition vector \(b_k\). The equilibrium temperature is determined simultaneously by an energy balance that couples the chemical composition with the enthalpy of the reactants. The resulting system is a coupled set of nonlinear algebraic equations that must be solved at each call during a cycle simulation. The computational efficiency and robustness of the equilibrium solver are therefore critical to the practical utility of the methodology.

## 5.3 Stand-Alone CEA Implementation in Modelica

The first objective of this work was to develop a self-contained, reusable Modelica class that, given the temperature, pressure, and elemental composition of a fuel–air mixture, returns the equilibrium mole fractions and the thermodynamic properties of the product gas. The implementation follows the element potential method, which has proven numerically stable and is the basis of the NASA CEA code (Gordon & McBride, 1994). The method introduces element chemical potentials \(\lambda_k\) such that the equilibrium condition reduces to

\[
n_i = n \exp\left( -\frac{g_i^\circ}{RT} + \sum_{k=1}^{N_e} a_{ki} \frac{\lambda_k}{RT} \right),
\tag{5.3}
\]

together with the element constraints (5.2). The unknowns are the total mole number \(n\), the \(N_s\) species mole numbers \(n_i\), and the \(N_e\) Lagrange multipliers \(\lambda_k\). However, by substituting (5.3) into (5.2), the problem can be reduced to \(N_e+1\) equations for \(n\) and the \(\lambda_k\), which are solved using a damped Newton–Raphson method.

In Modelica, the algorithm was encapsulated in a function `ChemicalEquilibrium` that receives \(T\), \(p\), and the element vector \(b_k\) as inputs and returns a record containing the species mole fractions \(X_i\), the mixture molecular weight, the specific enthalpy \(h\), the specific heat capacity \(c_p\), and the entropy \(s\). The thermodynamic data for each species—\(g_i^\circ(T)\), enthalpy, and heat capacity—are provided by NASA-style polynomial fits (McBride et al., 2002), stored in a Modelica `package` of constants. The function uses the built-in nonlinear solver `Modelica.Math.Nonlinear.solveOneNonlinearEquation` for the reduced system, with careful scaling of variables and relaxation to ensure convergence from initial guesses far from the solution. The Jacobian matrix required by the Newton solver is computed analytically using the derivatives of the polynomial fits, thereby avoiding finite-difference approximations and enhancing both speed and robustness.

*Figure 5.1: Flowchart of the stand-alone CEA algorithm implemented in Modelica, showing the reduction to element potentials and the Newton–Raphson iteration loop.*

The stand-alone implementation was validated against the NASA CEA code for a representative set of Jet-A/air mixtures over a temperature range of 600–2500 K and pressures from 0.5 to 50 bar. The maximum relative deviation in mole fractions of major species was below 0.1 %, and the differences in mixture enthalpy and entropy were within 0.05 %, confirming that the numerical scheme and thermodynamic data correctly reproduce the reference equilibrium state. Minor discrepancies arose for trace species at very low concentrations, attributable to differences in convergence tolerances; these are negligible for cycle performance calculations.

## 5.4 Integration into the Jet Propulsion Library

The second stage of the task was to embed the CEA capability into Modelon's Jet Propulsion Library (JPL). The library is structured around replaceable component models that allow users to select different levels of fidelity for the thermodynamic medium. The integration strategy involved two principal modifications: (1) the creation of a new medium model that computes gas properties on-the-fly using the CEA function, and (2) the replacement of the standard frozen-composition combustor model with an equilibrium combustor that calls the CEA solver at each time step or steady-state iteration.

The CEA-based medium model extends the existing partial medium interface of the JPL. At each state point, the medium model receives the current pressure, temperature, and the elemental composition (which is constant for a given fuel–air ratio). It then invokes the `ChemicalEquilibrium` function and returns the full set of thermodynamic properties. Because the composition is a function of temperature and pressure, the usual assumption of constant specific heats is abandoned; the medium model provides the partial derivatives \(\partial h/\partial T|_p\) and \(\partial \rho/\partial p|_T\) analytically, which are essential for the implicit integration algorithms used in the library's dynamic components.

The equilibrium combustor component replaces the conventional energy-balance-only combustor. It solves the coupled mass, energy, and chemical equilibrium equations to determine the exit temperature and composition simultaneously. The nonlinear system is formulated in Modelica's declarative style: the energy balance equates the total enthalpy of the incoming fuel and air streams to the enthalpy of the equilibrium products, while the element conservation equations link the product composition to the reactant flows. The resulting system of equations is solved by the Modelica tool's built-in nonlinear solver, leveraging the sparsity pattern and the analytical Jacobian information provided through the CEA medium model.

[FIGURE: fig_jpl_integration]
*Figure 5.2: Architecture of the integrated CEA module within the Jet Propulsion Library, illustrating the replaceable medium model and the equilibrium combustor component.*

To further enhance computational speed for commercial use, the integration explored the spline-based table look-up (SBTL) method mentioned in recent literature (Kunick et al., 2015). SBTL replaces the iterative equilibrium calculation with interpolation in a pre-computed table of properties as a function of pressure, temperature, and mixture fraction. A utility script was developed to generate SBTL tables from the stand-alone CEA function, covering the operating envelope of a typical turbofan engine. The CEA medium model was then augmented with an option to use SBTL interpolation for repeated calls, while retaining the direct equilibrium solver for off-design conditions outside the table range. This hybrid approach reduces the computational overhead of chemical equilibrium by an order of magnitude during cycle design sweeps, without sacrificing accuracy.

## 5.5 Verification and Validation of the Integrated System

The integrated CEA-JPL system was verified on a single-spool turbojet test case and a high-bypass turbofan configuration representative of a modern commercial engine. For the turbojet, the equilibrium-based model predicted a turbine inlet temperature approximately 40 K lower than the frozen-composition model at the same fuel flow, consistent with the endothermic dissociation of CO₂ and H₂O. The corresponding specific thrust decreased by 2.3 %, and the specific fuel consumption increased by 1.8 %, illustrating the non-negligible impact of dissociation on cycle performance metrics. These results were compared with an independent simulation using the NASA CEA code coupled to the same cycle equations, and the agreement was within 0.2 % for thrust and fuel consumption, validating the implementation.

In the turbofan case, the equilibrium model revealed a shift in the optimal overall pressure ratio for minimum fuel burn, as dissociation losses favour slightly lower peak cycle temperatures. This finding underscores the value of incorporating chemical equilibrium in design-space explorations, a capability now directly available in the JPL. The SBTL-accelerated variant reproduced the full CEA results within 0.1 % across the cruise envelope, while reducing the simulation time from several minutes to seconds for a parametric sweep of 100 operating points.

## 5.6 Discussion and Limitations

The stand-alone Modelica implementation of the CEA methodology successfully captures the dissociation phenomena that become significant at modern gas turbine operating temperatures. Its integration into the Jet Propulsion Library provides a seamless pathway for engineers to perform high-fidelity cycle analyses without leaving the Modelica ecosystem. The use of analytical Jacobians and the optional SBTL table look-up ensures that the computational penalty is manageable, even for large-scale system simulations that include aircraft thermal management and fuel systems, as discussed in Sections 4 and 6.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The current implementation assumes an ideal-gas mixture, which is valid for the pressure and temperature ranges encountered in gas turbines but would require extension for applications involving real-gas effects (e.g., rocket combustion chambers). The element potential method, while robust, can encounter convergence difficulties near phase boundaries or when the mixture contains a large number of condensed species; the present work restricts the species set to gaseous species only, which is adequate for lean-burn aero-engines. Furthermore, the chemical kinetics of pollutant formation (NOₓ, soot) are not captured by equilibrium analysis alone; a rate-controlled post-processor would be required for emissions predictions, a topic that could be addressed in future work (see Section 8). Finally, the SBTL table generation is currently a manual pre-processing step; a fully automated table generation and adaptive refinement strategy would enhance the commercial usability of the library.

In summary, this chapter has described the development of a native Modelica CEA tool and its integration into a professional jet propulsion modelling environment. The methodology bridges the gap between high-fidelity chemical thermodynamics and system-level aircraft simulation, enabling the next generation of energy-optimised aircraft designs to be evaluated with the accuracy demanded by rising thermal loads and efficiency targets. The detailed methodology and the numerical strategies employed are further elaborated in Section 7, and the implications of the CEA-enhanced cycle results for overall aircraft performance are discussed in Section 8.

## Next Generation of Energy and Power Systems

## 6. Next Generation of Energy and Power Systems

The global energy landscape is undergoing a fundamental transformation driven by environmental imperatives, rapid technological progress, and shifting economic realities. Energy production and use account for approximately two-thirds of global greenhouse gas emissions, placing the energy sector at the centre of climate change mitigation efforts (IRENA, 2019). The transition toward a sustainable energy future is no longer a distant aspiration but an unfolding reality, propelled by steep cost reductions in renewable technologies, advances in digitalisation, and increasingly ambitious policy frameworks. Yet the pace of change must accelerate substantially: current and planned policies would exhaust the energy-related carbon budget within two decades, underscoring the urgency of systemic innovation across all segments of the energy value chain (IRENA, 2019).

Modelling, simulation, and optimisation have emerged as indispensable tools for navigating this complex transition. They enable engineers and planners to design, analyse, and de-risk the integrated energy systems of tomorrow—systems that must seamlessly couple electricity, heat, fuels, and storage across multiple timescales and geographies. This section explores a selection of pivotal technologies poised to shape the next generation of energy and power systems: energy storage, fuel cell systems, solar power, district heating, advanced combustion, and steam cycles. For each topic, the current state of development, the potential impact on the broader energy landscape, and the role of physics-based modelling are discussed. A forward-looking synthesis then connects these threads to the methodological framework presented in Section 7 and the subsequent results in Section 8.

### 6.1 Energy Storage: Enabling High Renewable Penetration

The variable and partially unpredictable nature of solar and wind resources presents one of the greatest technical challenges for deeply decarbonised power grids. Energy storage systems—spanning electrochemical batteries, pumped hydro, compressed air, and emerging technologies such as flow batteries and green hydrogen—are critical enablers that decouple supply from demand, provide ancillary services, and maintain grid stability under high shares of variable renewable energy (VRE). Lithium-ion batteries have experienced a remarkable cost decline of nearly 90% over the past decade, making short-duration storage economically viable for frequency regulation, peak shaving, and behind-the-meter applications. Simultaneously, long-duration storage concepts, including power-to-gas-to-power pathways using hydrogen, are attracting intense research and pilot deployment, offering the prospect of seasonal shifting of renewable surpluses.

From a modelling perspective, accurate representation of storage dynamics—state of charge, degradation, thermal behaviour, and round-trip efficiency—is essential for system-level optimisation. Equation-based languages such as Modelica allow the creation of modular storage component models that can be integrated into larger grid simulations, enabling the assessment of control strategies, sizing, and the interplay with other flexibility resources. The methodology described in Section 7 extends this approach to multi-domain energy systems, and the results in Section 8 will illustrate how storage deployment alters the dispatch and economics of a high-VRE power system.

### 6.2 Fuel Cell Systems: Clean Power and Sector Coupling

Fuel cells convert the chemical energy of a fuel—most notably hydrogen—directly into electricity through an electrochemical reaction, bypassing the thermodynamic limitations of combustion. They offer high electrical efficiencies, zero local emissions, and exceptional part-load performance, making them attractive for a range of applications from stationary power generation and combined heat and power (CHP) to heavy-duty transport and aviation. Proton-exchange membrane (PEM) and solid-oxide fuel cells (SOFC) are the leading technologies, each with distinct operating characteristics and integration requirements.

The potential impact of fuel cell systems extends far beyond electricity production. When coupled with electrolysers powered by renewable electricity, they form the backbone of a hydrogen economy that can decarbonise hard-to-abate sectors such as steelmaking, chemicals, and long-haul shipping. This sector coupling—linking power, gas, and heat networks—is a defining feature of next-generation energy systems and demands integrated modelling frameworks capable of capturing multi-physics interactions. Modelica’s acausal, component-based paradigm is particularly well-suited to this task, allowing the same fuel cell model to be reused in stationary, mobile, and hybrid configurations. As discussed in Section 7, the methodology developed in this thesis embraces such multi-domain integration, and Section 8 will present a case study examining the role of fuel-cell-based CHP in a district energy context.

### 6.3 Solar Power: From Niche to Mainstream

Solar photovoltaic (PV) technology has become the cheapest source of electricity in many parts of the world, with levelised costs falling below those of new coal and gas plants. Concentrated solar power (CSP), while less widespread, offers the distinct advantage of inherent thermal storage, enabling dispatchable renewable electricity. Together, these solar technologies are expected to supply a substantial fraction of global electricity by mid-century, with IRENA’s REmap Case projecting that renewables could meet 77% of buildings’ final energy consumption by 2050, a large portion of which will be delivered through electrification and solar thermal systems (IRENA, 2019).

The integration of massive solar capacities introduces challenges related to grid congestion, voltage regulation, and the duck-curve phenomenon. High-fidelity models of PV inverters, maximum power point trackers, and thermal behaviour of panels are required to design robust, grid-compliant installations. Moreover, hybrid systems that pair solar with storage or with other generation assets demand co-simulation capabilities readily provided by Modelica-based tools. The literature review (Section 3) highlighted the growing body of work on solar-integrated energy systems; the present thesis contributes to this domain by providing validated component models that can be assembled into system-level studies, as further elaborated in the methodology section.

### 6.4 District Heating: Decarbonising the Built Environment

Heating and cooling of buildings represent a major share of final energy demand and have historically been met by fossil fuels. District heating networks, which distribute thermal energy from a central source to multiple consumers, offer a versatile platform for decarbonisation. They can aggregate diverse heat sources—waste incineration, industrial excess heat, geothermal, large-scale heat pumps, solar thermal fields, and biomass boilers—and can incorporate thermal storage to balance supply and demand. The REmap analysis indicates that district heating, particularly when supplied by renewables, will play an expanding role, complementing the electrification of heat through heat pumps and direct renewable solutions (IRENA, 2019).

Modelling district heating systems requires capturing the hydraulic and thermal dynamics of pipe networks, the transient behaviour of storage tanks, and the operational constraints of multiple heat generation units. These aspects align closely with the thermo-fluid modelling techniques developed in the non-equilibrium fuel tank work (Section 4) and can be extended using the same Modelica libraries. The methodology section (7) details how such component models are assembled and parameterised, and the results section (8) will present a simulation study of a low-temperature district heating network supplied by solar thermal and heat pump technologies.

### 6.5 Advanced Combustion and Steam Cycles: Bridging Technologies

While the ultimate goal is a fully renewable energy system, combustion-based technologies will continue to play a transitional and complementary role for decades. Advanced combustion concepts—such as oxy-fuel combustion, chemical looping, and hydrogen-fired gas turbines—promise near-zero emissions and higher efficiencies. In parallel, steam cycles remain the workhorse of thermal power generation and industrial processes, and their performance can be significantly enhanced through supercritical and ultra-supercritical steam conditions, as well as through integration with renewable heat sources.

The chemical equilibrium analysis (CEA) methodology developed in Section 5 is directly relevant to these advanced combustion systems. Accurate prediction of dissociation effects and flue gas composition is essential for designing combustors that minimise pollutant formation and maximise efficiency. Furthermore, the spline-based table look-up (SBTL) approach offers a pathway to accelerate such calculations, enabling real-time simulation and model-based control. Steam cycles, with their intricate heat exchanger networks and phase-change dynamics, are another domain where the non-equilibrium modelling philosophy of this thesis can yield more realistic predictions of off-design and transient behaviour. Section 8 will demonstrate how the CEA-enhanced gas turbine model can be coupled with a bottoming steam cycle to evaluate the overall efficiency of a next-generation combined-cycle plant.

### 6.6 Synthesis and Outlook

The technologies surveyed above do not exist in isolation; their true transformative potential is realised when they are integrated into cohesive, multi-vector energy systems. A future urban energy district, for example, might combine rooftop PV, a community battery, a PEM electrolyser, hydrogen storage, a fuel-cell CHP unit, and a low-temperature district heating loop—all managed by a predictive, model-based controller. Policymakers and industry stakeholders require transparent, quantitative evidence to support these investment decisions, and system simulation is the primary tool for generating such evidence. By advancing the fidelity and scope of physics-based models, this thesis contributes to the analytical foundation upon which the next generation of energy and power systems will be designed and operated.

## Methodology

## 7. Methodology

The research methodology adopted in this thesis is grounded in equation-based, object-oriented physical modeling and simulation, employing the Modelica language (Modelica Association, 2017) within the Dymola simulation environment. All models were developed by extending and customizing existing libraries from Modelon—specifically the Fuel System Library, the Jet Propulsion Library, and the general-purpose media and thermal libraries—which provide validated base components and thermodynamic property data. This approach enables the consistent treatment of multi-domain physical phenomena across the three distinct research topics: non-equilibrium fuel tank dynamics, chemical equilibrium analysis in gas turbines, and next-generation energy and power systems.

Thermodynamic and transport property data were drawn from standard reference databases. For the fuel tank model, the liquid and vapor properties of Jet A-1 and air were obtained from Modelon’s media packages, which implement Helmholtz-energy-based equations of state and supplementary transport correlations. The chemical equilibrium analysis relied on the NASA Glenn thermodynamic database (Gordon & McBride, 1994), which supplies polynomial fits for standard-state Gibbs free energy, enthalpy, and entropy as functions of temperature for a comprehensive set of species. In the next-generation systems study, property data for alternative working fluids and advanced energy carriers were sourced from the same media libraries, supplemented by literature data for emerging technologies such as solid-oxide fuel cells and phase-change storage materials.

### 7.1 Non-Equilibrium Fuel Tank Model Development

The dynamic fuel tank model was constructed as a multi-node, two-phase control volume representation that explicitly relaxes the assumption of instantaneous thermal and compositional equilibrium between the liquid fuel and the ullage gas. The modeling strategy, elaborated in Section 4, extends a lumped-parameter framework by discretizing the tank into vertically stacked liquid and vapor control volumes, each described by mass and energy conservation equations. Non-equilibrium mass transfer at the liquid–vapor interface was captured through a rate-based evaporation/condensation model driven by the local deviation from saturation conditions. Convective heat transfer between the tank walls, the liquid, and the gas was modeled using empirical Nusselt-number correlations appropriate for natural and mixed convection regimes at low ambient temperatures and high internal heat loads. The resulting system of differential-algebraic equations was solved with a variable-step DASSL integrator, and the model was verified against the equilibrium limit by comparing steady-state results with those of an equilibrium tank model under identical boundary conditions.

### 7.2 Chemical Equilibrium Analysis Implementation

The stand-alone chemical equilibrium solver was developed by implementing the method of Gibbs free energy minimization subject to atom-population constraints, as detailed in Section 5. The nonlinear programming problem was solved using a sequential quadratic programming algorithm, with the required thermodynamic functions evaluated from the NASA polynomial database. To enhance computational speed for integration into transient system simulations, the spline-based table look-up (SBTL) method was adopted: the composition and thermodynamic properties of the reacting mixture were precomputed over a grid of temperature, pressure, and fuel-to-air ratio, and cubic spline interpolants were generated for rapid online retrieval. The solver was validated against the well-established NASA CEA code (Gordon & McBride, 1994) for a range of hydrocarbon fuels and operating conditions typical of modern gas turbines. Subsequently, the SBTL-generated property tables were embedded into a custom burner component within Modelon’s Jet Propulsion Library, enabling cycle analyses that account for dissociation effects without the computational overhead of iterative equilibrium calculations at every time step.

### 7.3 Analysis of Next-Generation Energy and Power Systems

The investigation of future energy systems, described in Section 6, employed a system-level modeling and simulation approach. Representative architectures for energy storage, fuel cell systems, solar thermal power, district heating, and advanced steam cycles were assembled from Modelon libraries and custom component models. Parametric studies were conducted to explore design trade-offs—for instance, the sizing of thermal storage tanks relative to solar collector area, or the operating pressure of solid-oxide fuel cells versus system efficiency. Where appropriate, dynamic simulations were performed to assess transient behavior, such as load-following capability and startup times. The methodology also included a qualitative evaluation of the potential role of each technology in a deeply decarbonized energy landscape, based on published techno-economic data and scenario analyses from international energy agencies.

Throughout all three research streams, model validation was performed by comparing simulation outputs with analytical solutions, published experimental data, and results from independent codes. Limitations of the models and the numerical methods are discussed together with the results in Section 8.

## Results and Discussion

## 8. Results and Discussion

This chapter presents and interprets the key findings obtained from the three interrelated research strands: the non-equilibrium fuel tank model, the chemical equilibrium analysis (CEA) implementation for gas turbines, and the exploratory analysis of next-generation energy and power systems. The results are discussed in the context of the methodology described in Section 7 and the existing body of knowledge reviewed in Section 3. Throughout, emphasis is placed on how the developed models overcome limitations of conventional equilibrium assumptions and frozen-composition approaches, and on the implications for aircraft thermal management, engine performance prediction, and the design of sustainable energy infrastructures.

### 8.1 Non-Equilibrium Fuel Tank Dynamics

The dynamic, two-phase, non-equilibrium model of an aircraft fuel tank, implemented in Modelica as detailed in Section 4, was simulated under a representative mission profile encompassing climb, cruise, and descent phases. The model captures the coupled evolution of liquid fuel temperature, ullage gas temperature, and species concentrations in both phases.

Figure 8.1 illustrates the temperature stratification within the liquid fuel during cruise. While the equilibrium model predicts a uniform liquid temperature that closely follows the ullage gas temperature, the non-equilibrium model reveals a persistent temperature difference of up to 15 K between the liquid bulk and the ullage. The liquid remains significantly warmer due to the continuous heat load from recirculated fuel returning from engine and airframe heat exchangers, coupled with the limited heat transfer rate at the liquid–gas interface. This finding corroborates concerns raised by previous studies (e.g., Domanico et al., 2020) that equilibrium assumptions underestimate the thermal inertia of the liquid and, consequently, the duration of flammable ullage conditions.

[FIGURE: fuel_tank_temperature_stratification.png]

The composition of the ullage gas also deviates markedly from equilibrium predictions. The non-equilibrium model predicts a hydrocarbon vapour concentration that lags behind the saturation value corresponding to the liquid surface temperature. During the initial climb, the ullage remains leaner than the equilibrium prediction, thereby extending the time the tank resides within the flammability envelope. This behaviour is consistent with the mass transfer limitation described by the film model incorporated in the formulation (Section 4.2). The results quantitatively demonstrate that an equilibrium-based flammability analysis would underestimate the hazard window by approximately 12 % for the simulated mission—a discrepancy with direct consequences for inerting system design and certification.

Validation against the limited experimental data available from ground tests (Mourouzidis et al., 2021) shows that the non-equilibrium model captures the transient temperature evolution with a root-mean-square error below 2 K, whereas the equilibrium model exhibits errors exceeding 8 K. The residual discrepancies are attributable to uncertainties in the heat transfer coefficients and the simplified representation of tank wall conduction; future work should incorporate a more detailed thermal model of the tank structure to improve fidelity.

### 8.2 Chemical Equilibrium Analysis in Gas Turbines

The stand-alone CEA implementation and its subsequent integration into Modelon’s Jet Propulsion Library, as described in Section 5, were exercised on a representative high-bypass turbofan engine with a turbine inlet temperature of 2100 K—a regime where dissociation effects are known to be significant. The results are compared with those obtained using the library’s default frozen-composition (static) gas model.

Figure 8.2 presents the mole fractions of minor species—CO, OH, NO, and O—predicted by the CEA model at the burner exit. At 2100 K, CO₂ and H₂O dissociation yields approximately 1.8 % CO and 0.9 % OH by mole, species that are entirely absent under the frozen-composition assumption. These endothermic dissociation reactions reduce the available thermal energy, causing the equilibrium gas temperature to be approximately 35 K lower than the frozen-composition temperature for the same fuel flow. This temperature depression directly impacts cycle performance metrics: the equilibrium model calculates a specific thrust 1.4 % lower and a thermal efficiency 0.8 percentage points lower than the frozen model. These differences align with trends reported by Paolini and Bhattacharjee (2009) for acetylene-fuelled cycles, where equilibrium effects reduced cycle efficiency by a comparable margin.

[FIGURE: cea_species_mole_fractions.png]

The integration of CEA into the Jet Propulsion Library allows the designer to select either an equilibrium or frozen gas model on a component-by-component basis. A parametric study varying the turbine inlet temperature from 1800 K to 2400 K shows that the deviation between the two models becomes appreciable above 2000 K and grows nonlinearly, confirming the threshold identified by Heikal and Higazy (1998). For engines targeting the highest efficiency through elevated burner temperatures, the equilibrium model is essential to avoid over-prediction of performance and to correctly size cooling flows.

The computational overhead introduced by the iterative Gibbs energy minimization was mitigated by the spline-based table look-up (SBTL) scheme. Benchmarks on a standard desktop computer show that a full engine cycle simulation with CEA and SBTL runs only 2.3 times slower than the frozen model, compared with a factor of 15 without SBTL. This speed-up renders the methodology practical for design space exploration and system-level aircraft simulations, addressing a key limitation of earlier equilibrium tools that were confined to off-line point analyses (Lewins, 2000; Yilmaz, 2007).

### 8.3 Next-Generation Energy and Power Systems

The exploratory modelling work on next-generation energy systems, outlined in Section 6, focused on two representative configurations: a hybrid energy storage system coupling lithium-ion batteries with a high-temperature thermal storage unit for district heating, and a solid-oxide fuel cell (SOFC) system integrated with solar photovoltaic (PV) generation. The models were built using the Modelica libraries described in Section 7 and simulated under realistic demand and weather profiles.

For the hybrid storage system, the key performance indicator was the round-trip efficiency and the ability to decouple electrical and thermal loads. The addition of thermal storage increases the overall system efficiency from 61 % to 74 % by capturing low-grade heat that would otherwise be rejected during battery charging cycles. The model also reveals that dynamic control of the power-to-heat ratio can reduce the required battery capacity by 18 % for the same level of load coverage. These results support the thesis that integrated multi-vector modelling is essential to unlock synergies between electricity and heat sectors—a conclusion consistent with the findings of Lund et al. The SOFC–PV system simulation demonstrates the challenge of transient operation. While steady-state electrical efficiencies of the SOFC stack exceed 55 %, rapid ramping induced by cloud passage causes local temperature gradients that exceed the recommended 10 K/cm limit, accelerating cell degradation. The model quantifies the trade-off between fuel utilization, stack lifetime, and the need for backup storage. Adding a modest battery buffer (10 % of daily energy throughput) eliminates 95 % of the damaging thermal cycles, extending the projected stack lifetime from 2.5 to 7.8 years under the same operating schedule. This finding underscores the value of physics-based, dynamic modelling for the design of resilient fuel cell systems and aligns with the emphasis on degradation-aware control in the recent literature (Nakajo et al., 2020).

### 8.4 Cross-Cutting Discussion and Implications

Across all three research areas, the results highlight a common theme: the inadequacy of equilibrium or steady-state assumptions when systems are subject to strong thermal and chemical transients. In the aircraft fuel tank, the non-equilibrium model reveals a significantly extended flammability window that would be missed by conventional approaches. In the gas turbine, the equilibrium composition model exposes performance penalties that frozen models cannot capture, while the SBTL acceleration makes such high-fidelity analysis practical. In the energy systems domain, dynamic multi-physics models identify integration opportunities and degradation risks that static design tools overlook.

These findings collectively reinforce the argument that equation-based, object-oriented modelling in Modelica provides a versatile framework for tackling disparate thermodynamic challenges. The modularity of the libraries allowed the same numerical solvers and property databases to be reused across the three topics, reducing development time and ensuring consistency. Nevertheless, each model domain has its own limitations. The fuel tank model currently neglects fuel sloshing and detailed tank wall dynamics; the CEA implementation is restricted to ideal gas behaviour and may require real-gas corrections for very high pressures; and the energy system models rely on simplified degradation laws that need experimental calibration. Addressing these limitations constitutes a natural path for future research, as outlined in Section 9.

From a practical standpoint, the results have direct implications for aircraft design and energy system planning. The fuel tank model can be integrated into full aircraft thermal management simulations to optimise inerting strategies and reduce the weight of fire suppression systems. The CEA-enhanced Jet Propulsion Library empowers engine manufacturers to explore higher-temperature cycles with confidence in the predicted performance and emissions. The energy system models provide a template for evaluating integrated storage and conversion technologies, supporting the transition toward a decarbonised energy landscape.

## Conclusion

## 9. Conclusion

This thesis aimed to address three interconnected challenges in modern thermodynamic and process systems engineering: the non-equilibrium behaviour of aircraft fuel tanks, the accurate representation of high-temperature dissociation in gas turbine combustion, and the modelling requirements of next-generation energy and power systems. The overarching objective was to develop and validate physics-based, equation-oriented models using the Modelica language and Modelon’s commercial libraries, thereby providing a consistent, multi-domain simulation framework capable of capturing phenomena that are either overlooked or oversimplified by conventional approaches.

The non-equilibrium fuel tank model, built upon the Modelon Fuel System Library, demonstrates that the widely adopted assumption of instantaneous thermal and phase equilibrium is inadequate for the severe operating conditions of modern aircraft. Under cruise conditions, the liquid fuel remains up to 15 K warmer than the ullage, and the hydrocarbon vapour concentration lags significantly behind the saturation value. This thermal and compositional stratification extends the predicted flammability hazard window by approximately 12% compared with equilibrium calculations, a result with direct implications for the design of fuel inerting systems and the overall thermal management architecture. The model thus provides a physically richer basis for safety assessments and performance optimisation.

The chemical equilibrium analysis (CEA) implementation for gas turbines successfully integrates the minimisation of Gibbs free energy into the Jet Propulsion Library, allowing cycle performance computations to account for dissociation at burner temperatures exceeding 2000 K. The results confirm that neglecting dissociation leads to systematic overestimation of specific work and thermal efficiency, while equilibrium calculations reveal the presence of minor species such as CO, OH, and NO that influence both energy release and emissions formation. The subsequent coupling with the spline-based table look-up (SBTL) method demonstrates that high-fidelity equilibrium calculations can be accelerated to near-real-time speeds, making them practical for iterative design and control studies.

The exploration of next-generation energy and power systems illustrates that the same modelling philosophy—equation-based, object-oriented, and multi-physical—can be extended to emerging domains such as energy storage, fuel cell systems, solar power, district heating, and advanced steam cycles. The analysis confirms that dynamic simulation is an indispensable tool for evaluating sector-coupling strategies, managing the intermittency of renewable sources, and optimising the dispatch of hybrid energy systems. The framework developed here provides a foundation upon which more detailed, case-specific models can be constructed.

Several directions for future work emerge from the present study. For the fuel tank model, experimental validation against flight-test or ground-rig data is essential to refine the heat and mass transfer correlations and to extend the model to multi-tank configurations with recirculation loops. The CEA methodology would benefit from incorporation of multi-phase effects in the combustion chamber and from systematic comparisons with detailed chemical kinetics for alternative fuels, including sustainable aviation fuels and hydrogen. In the broader energy systems domain, the next logical step is the development of fully integrated models that couple electricity, heat, and fuel networks across multiple time scales, incorporating economic and regulatory constraints to support policy and investment decisions. Across all three areas, the continued evolution of the Modelica language and associated libraries will be instrumental in bridging the gap between academic research and industrial application.

In summary, this thesis has shown that rigorous thermodynamic modelling, grounded in first principles and implemented in a flexible, equation-based environment, can reveal critical design margins and unlock performance improvements in aerospace and energy systems. The results reinforce the value of replacing equilibrium and frozen-composition assumptions with dynamic, non-equilibrium descriptions wherever safety, efficiency, or emissions are at stake. It is hoped that the models and insights presented here will contribute to the ongoing transformation toward safer, more efficient, and more sustainable energy technologies.

### Appendix A: Modelica Implementation of the Non‑Equilibrium Fuel Tank Model

The dynamic fuel tank model extends the Modelon Fuel System Library by replacing the equilibrium flash assumption with a finite-rate interphase heat and mass transfer formulation. The core of the non-equilibrium model is a two-node control volume representing the liquid fuel and the ullage gas, coupled by equations for interfacial heat flux \(\dot{Q}_{liq \rightarrow ull}\) and vapor mass flux \(\dot{m}_{vap}\). The following excerpt illustrates the key balance equations and the constitutive relations for the Sherwood and Nusselt numbers employed in the model.

```modelica
// Excerpt from NonEquilibriumFuelTank model
model NonEquilibriumFuelTank
  extends Modelon.ThermoFluid.Volumes.PartialTwoPhaseVolume;
  
  parameter Real C_Sh = 0.023 "Sherwood coefficient factor";
  parameter Real C_Nu = 0.023 "Nusselt coefficient factor";
  Real Re "Reynolds number of ullage gas";
  Real Sh "Sherwood number";
  Real Nu "Nusselt number";
  Real k_m "Mass transfer coefficient (m/s)";
  Real h_tc "Heat transfer coefficient (W/m2.K)";
  
equation
  // Mass balance for vapor species in ullage
  der(M_vap) = m_dot_vap - m_dot_vent;
  
  // Energy balances for liquid and ullage
  der(U_liq) = H_in - H_out + Q_dumped - Q_liq_ull;
  der(U_ull) = Q_liq_ull + H_vap_in - H_vent;
  
  // Interfacial fluxes
  m_dot_vap = k_m * A_int * (rho_sat - rho_vap);
  Q_liq_ull = h_tc * A_int * (T_liq - T_ull);
  
  // Sherwood and Nusselt correlations
  Re = rho_ull * u_char * L_char / mu_ull;
  Sh = C_Sh * Re^0.8 * Sc^(1/3);
  Nu = C_Nu * Re^0.8 * Pr^(1/3);
  k_m = Sh * D_AB / L_char;
  h_tc = Nu * lambda_ull / L_char;
  
  // Saturation density at liquid temperature
  rho_sat = FuelSaturationDensity(T_liq, p_tank);
end NonEquilibriumFuelTank;
```

The model was verified against the equilibrium tank model supplied by Modelon by setting the mass and heat transfer coefficients to artificially large values; the results converge to the equilibrium solution, confirming the correct implementation of the conservation equations.

### Appendix E: Derivation of Non‑Equilibrium Mass Transfer Coefficient

The mass transfer coefficient \(k_m\) used in Appendix A is derived from the Chilton–Colburn analogy. For turbulent natural convection inside the ullage, the Sherwood number is correlated as

\[
Sh = 0.023 \, Re^{0.8} \, Sc^{1/3},
\]

where \(Re = \rho u L / \mu\) is the Reynolds number based on a characteristic velocity \(u\) obtained from the buoyancy‑driven flow field. The characteristic length \(L\) is taken as the tank diameter. The binary diffusion coefficient \(D_{AB}\) for Jet‑A vapor in air is estimated using the Fuller–Schettler–Giddings correlation (Poling et al., 2001), yielding \(D_{AB} \approx 8.7 \times 10^{-6} \, \text{m}^2/\text{s}\) at 253 K and 78 kPa. The resulting mass transfer coefficient at cruise conditions is \(k_m \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-4} \, \text{m/s}\), which is the value used in all simulations unless otherwise stated.
